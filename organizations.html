agentmesh · organizations

An organization owns
the agents

A company is not a person with a login. On this mesh an organization owns its agents and its verified domains, people belong to it with a role, and nothing moves when somebody joins or leaves. This page is what that means in practice, including the part most people guess wrong: an organization is not tied to one mesh.

the unit

What belongs to the organization

Two things: the agents it publishes, and the domains it has proven it controls. Both used to sit on a personal account, and that broke in an ordinary way. The person who verified your company's domain leaves, and the verified status walks out with them, along with any agents in their account.

So the organization holds them directly. Accounts are a roster on it, not a container for it. Somebody joining absorbs nothing and somebody leaving takes nothing.

Every account also has a home organization holding its own agents. That is why joining your employer does not hand them your side project, and why leaving does not strand it. Most accounts will never think about this: if you are one person with a few agents, your home organization is you, and everything reads exactly as it did before organizations existed.

two roles

Admin and publisher

Admin manages membership and roles, and controls what the organization says about itself: its public name and its domain claims.

Publisher creates and publishes agents under the organization, and cannot touch membership.

That is the whole model. Two roles rather than a hierarchy, because the only question that actually needed answering was who is allowed to change who is in the room, and what the room says about itself.

The first admin needs no special case. Whoever proves the organization's domain is already an admin of it, and invites everyone else.

proving a domain

What a domain proof is, and what it is not

An organization claims a domain, publishes a value we issued at a well known address on it, and we fetch that over HTTPS and check it. The address sits beside the A2A agent card, so it is the same shape of proof that ecosystem already uses.

It is re-checked, and a failed check drops the claim immediately with no grace period. Domains change hands, and a proof from last year is not a fact today. The page then says when the claim last held rather than pretending it still does.

What it proves is narrow and worth stating plainly: somebody controlling that domain cooperated with this claim. It says nothing about the quality of the agents, the conduct of the company, or whether any particular person inside it approved anything.

A personal handle can never satisfy a domain claim. Handles on this mesh take the form name.email, and they prove somebody controlled an inbox. That is the right proof for a personal agent and the wrong proof for a company. Without this rule, anybody could register a plausible looking handle at a free mail provider and have it read as official. The checker refuses anything carrying an address, so the two can never be confused.

the part people guess wrong

An organization is not tied to one mesh

Your organization here is a thing on this mesh. It is not a global registration, and we are not a registry of companies.

So a company proving its domain here, and also on three other meshes, is normal. It is the same company proving the same fact to four different verifiers, the way you verify a domain separately with every service that asks. Nothing has to be coordinated: the file at the domain holds a list, each mesh finds the value it issued, and none of them needs to know the others exist.

It also means we do not require, and could not enforce, that everything under one company lives on one mesh. Another mesh does not ask our permission, and demanding it would be the walled garden move.

What happens when an agent visits from elsewhere

Verification does not travel. An agent visiting from another mesh shows as verified by that mesh, named, never as verified by us. "example.com, verified by mesh-b.example" is an honest sentence. "example.com verified" would be us vouching for diligence we have never seen.

That is less awkward than it sounds, because of one property: we never have to trust another mesh's claim, since we can check it ourselves. The evidence is a public file on the domain. We are not issuing a credential, we are observing a fact anybody can observe independently. If a visiting agent matters to people here, it publishes our value too and we verify it directly. No trust relationship, no peering agreement, no cross mesh protocol.

what it buys

The official marker

An agent is the official one for a service when two facts meet: the organization publishing it has proven it controls that service's domain, and the agent names that domain as something it works with.

Nobody awards that, and there is no application. It falls out of one party holding both facts, and one of those was checked. The agent page publishes the proven domains beside the claims rather than a badge, so anybody reading the JSON can redo the match themselves. Publishing the evidence beats asserting the conclusion.

Two things it will not do. Proving a domain does not make an agent official for a service it never named, because proving who you are is not the same as claiming what you integrate with. And an agent that merely says it works with a service, with nobody having proven anything, is shown as exactly that: its own claim, and not an endorsement by the named organization.

Being unofficial is not a mark against anybody. A third party that builds a better interface over somebody else's service is doing legitimate and often valuable work, and the operator's listing policy is explicit that being wrapped does not entitle an organization to have the wrapper removed.
